Newton's second law: F = ma

How net force, mass and acceleration are linked, and how to solve the harder problems with ropes, friction, cables and elevators. About 90 minutes. Split it across two evenings.

Before you start

Have these ready:

  • Calculator
  • Pencil and paper for FBDs
  • Your Newton’s Second Law notes, the Newton’s Laws worksheet and the problem-solving sheet from Google Classroom

Lessons 12 to 14 first. Use g = 9.8 m/s². Kinematics (Big Five) shows up again when a problem gives distance or time instead of acceleration.

1The second law

The first law says no net force means no change in velocity. The second law says what happens when there is a net force.

Newton’s second law

F⃗net = m a⃗   or   a⃗ = F⃗net ÷ m

  • More net force gives more acceleration (directly proportional).
  • More mass gives less acceleration (a bowling ball is harder to speed up than a tennis ball).
  • The acceleration points in the same direction as the net force.

Units: 1 newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s². So 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². For the answer to come out in N, the mass must be in kg and the acceleration in m/s². (Grams must be converted: 100 g = 0.100 kg.)

The law has two uses: predict the motion when you know the forces, or learn about the forces by watching the motion.

2The method for every force problem

  1. Sketch and draw the FBD. Write the mass and the direction of motion on it.
  2. Choose a positive direction. Pick the direction of the acceleration if you know it.
  3. Write Fnet = ma for that direction and substitute the force terms with signs (+ along the positive direction, − against it).
  4. Solve. If the problem gives distance or time instead of acceleration, find a first with the Big Five.
  5. Sense check: does the direction of Fnet match the motion (speeding up) or oppose it (slowing down)?
Watch out

Do not put ma on the FBD as a force. It is the result of the forces: the net force. Show it separately as the squiggly acceleration arrow.

3Worked examples: straight along a surface

Worked example 1: a 1.2 kg cart accelerates at 0.50 m/s² [right]. What net force acts on it?

Given: m = 1.2 kg, a = +0.50 m/s² (right positive).
Step 1. Fnet = ma = 1.2 × 0.50 = 0.60 N [right].

Worked example 2: a net force of 25 N [W] acts on a 100 g mass. What is the acceleration?

Convert first: 100 g = 0.100 kg.
Step 1. a = Fnet ÷ m = 25 ÷ 0.100 = 250 m/s² [W].
Step 2. Sense check. A small mass with a big force gives a huge acceleration. ✓
Using 100 instead of 0.100 would give 0.25, a thousand times too small.

Worked example 3: a 75 N [forward] force pushes a 50 kg crate. Friction of 45 N opposes the motion. What is the acceleration?

Forward is positive. Forces along the floor: +75 and −45.
Step 1. Fnet = 75 − 45 = +30 N.
Step 2. a = 30 ÷ 50 = 0.60 m/s² [forward].

Worked example 4: a 20 kg box slides on a smooth floor. One person pushes with 80 N [forward]. The box slows down at 1.5 m/s² [backward]. What force does the other person push with?

Forward positive. The acceleration is backward, so a = −1.5 m/s². Let F2 be the other person’s force, pushing backward.
Step 1. Fnet = ma = 20 × (−1.5) = −30 N.
Step 2. Fnet = 80 − F2 = −30.
Step 3. F2 = 110 N [backward].

4Ropes and friction

Friction opposes the motion. For a surface it is often given as a coefficient: Ff = μFn. On a level floor with no vertical forces other than Fg, Fn = mg.

Worked example 5: a 23 kg sled is pulled along snow by a rope. The coefficient of friction is 0.14 and the sled accelerates at 2.7 m/s². Find the tension.

Right positive. Vertical: Fn = mg = 23 × 9.8 = 225.4 N (the rope is horizontal).
Step 1. Friction. Ff = μFn = 0.14 × 225.4 = 31.6 N.
Step 2. Horizontal net force. Ft − Ff = ma.
Step 3. Ft = ma + Ff = 23 × 2.7 + 31.6 = 93.7, so 94 N.

Worked example 6: a 25 kg skater is pulled from rest to 5.0 m/s in 12 s on a rope. Friction is negligible. Find the tension.

Given: m = 25 kg, vi = 0, vf = 5.0 m/s, Δt = 12 s.
Step 1. Acceleration first (kinematics). a = (5.0 − 0) ÷ 12 = 0.417 m/s².
Step 2. Net force. Ft = ma = 25 × 0.417 = 10.4, so 10 N.

5Hanging, lifting and elevators

When something hangs from a rope or cable, the rope pulls up (tension) and gravity pulls down. Take up as positive.

Tension in a cable, up positive

Fnet = Ft − mg = ma  ⇒  Ft = m(g + a)

MotionAccelerationTension
At rest or constant velocity (up or down)a = 0Ft = mg
Speeding up while going up, or slowing down while going downa upFt > mg
Slowing down while going up, or speeding up while going downa downFt < mg

Use a positive value of a when it is up and a negative value when it is down.

Worked example 7: a 170 kg container is lowered by a cable. Find the tension (a) at constant speed, (b) when it accelerates downward at 2.1 m/s².

Up is positive. mg = 170 × 9.8 = 1666 N.
(a) a = 0, so Ft = mg = 1700 N (1666 N).
(b) a = −2.1 m/s²: Ft − 1666 = 170 × (−2.1), so Ft = 1666 − 357 = 1300 N.
Sense check. The cable pulls less than the weight when the load speeds up downward. ✓
If the cable pulled with exactly mg, the load would not accelerate.

Worked example 8: an elevator and its load have a mass of 1600 kg and move down at 12 m/s. They stop in 42 m with constant acceleration. Find the tension in the cable.

Up is positive. vi = −12 m/s, vf = 0, Δd = −42 m.
Step 1. Acceleration (Big Five, no time given). a = (vf² − vi²) ÷ (2Δd) = (0 − 144) ÷ (−84) = +1.71 m/s² (up).
Step 2. Net force. Ft − mg = ma, so Ft = 1600 × (9.8 + 1.71) = 18 400 N, or 1.8 × 10⁴ N.
Step 3. Sense check. It is slowing while going down, so a is up and Ft is greater than mg (15 680 N). ✓

6Two objects pulled together

When objects are tied together and move with the same acceleration, treat one at a time with its own FBD. The rope or link closest to the pulling force carries the force for everything behind it.

Worked example 9: two 2.4 kg buckets hang one below the other. The top rope lifts both with an acceleration of 1.0 m/s² [up]. Find the tension in each rope.

Up positive, a = +1.0.
Step 1. Bottom rope holds only the bottom bucket: Ft,bottom = m(g + a) = 2.4 × 10.8 = 25.9, so 26 N.
Step 2. Top rope holds both buckets (4.8 kg): Ft,top = 4.8 × 10.8 = 51.8, so 52 N.
Step 3. Check. The top rope is exactly twice the bottom one because it lifts twice the mass. ✓

Worked example 10: a train pulls two 8500 kg cars from rest to 60 km/h in 45 s. Find the tension in the link between the train and the first car, and between the cars. (No friction.)

Convert: 60 km/h = 16.7 m/s. a = 16.7 ÷ 45 = 0.370 m/s².
Step 1. Link between the cars pulls only the second car: T = 8500 × 0.370 = 3150, so 3100 N.
Step 2. Link at the train pulls both cars (17 000 kg): T = 17 000 × 0.370 = 6290, so 6300 N.

7Knowledge check

8Hands-on: feel F = ma

Lift a bottle on a rubber band or string
  1. Hang a water bottle from a rubber band (or a spring or a thin elastic cord) and hold it still. Notice how much the band stretches.
  2. Lift the bottle quickly upward, speeding up. Does the band stretch more or less?
  3. Stop the bottle quickly while lifting, then lower it fast and stop. What happens to the stretch?
Show a model answer

At rest the stretch matches the weight: Ft = mg. When I sped up upward, the acceleration was up, so Ft > mg and the band stretched more. When I slowed down while going up (or sped up while going down), the acceleration was down, so Ft < mg and the band stretched less. The weight never changed. Only the acceleration did.
