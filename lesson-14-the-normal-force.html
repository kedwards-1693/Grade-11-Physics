The normal force

What a surface pushes back with, why it is not always equal to weight, and how to find it when other forces act. About 75 minutes.

Before you start

Have these ready:

  • Calculator in degree mode
  • Pencil, ruler and paper for FBD sketches
  • Your Normal Force worksheet and slides from Google Classroom

You need Lessons 12 and 13 (net force, free body diagrams) and Lesson 8 (components) for this one. Use g = 9.8 N/kg, so Fg = mg.

1What is the normal force?

The normal force (Fn) is the push of a surface on an object that is touching it. “Normal” here means perpendicular: the force is always at right angles to the surface, pointing away from it.

  • On a floor or table, Fn points up.
  • On a wall, Fn points sideways, away from the wall.
  • On a ceiling, Fn points down, away from the ceiling.

It exists because the surface is slightly squashed, like a very stiff spring, and pushes back. It also only exists while there is contact.

Watch out

The normal force is not always equal to the weight. It is equal to the weight only when nothing else pushes or pulls vertically and the object is not accelerating up or down. Always get it from the net force equation.

2Finding F_n from the net force

Use the same recipe every time: draw the FBD, choose up as positive, and write the vertical net force.

Flat surface, nothing else vertical

Fnet,y = Fn − Fg = 0  ⇒  Fn = Fg = mg

Worked example 1: a 0.50 kg book rests on your outstretched hand. What is the normal force?

Given: m = 0.50 kg; a = 0. Up is positive.
Step 1. FBD. Fn up, Fg down.
Step 2. Net force. Fn − Fg = 0, so Fn = Fg.
Step 3. Solve. Fn = 0.50 × 9.8 = 4.9 N [up].

Worked example 2: the hand now speeds up upward and the net force on the book is 3.0 N [up]. What is the normal force?

Given: Fnet = +3.0 N, Fg = 4.9 N. The net force is up, so the acceleration is up.
Step 1. Fnet,y = Fn − Fg = 3.0.
Step 2. Fn = 3.0 + 4.9 = 7.9 N.
Step 3. Compare. Fn (7.9 N) is bigger than Fg (4.9 N).
Whenever the acceleration is up, Fn > Fg. This is why you feel heavier when an elevator starts to go up.
The three cases for the book on the hand

Hand at rest or moving at constant velocity: a = 0, so Fn = Fg.

Hand moving up and speeding up: a is up, so Fn > Fg (Fg stays the same).

Hand moving up but slowing down: a is down, so Fn < Fg (Fg stays the same).

Moving up or down does not matter on its own. Only the acceleration decides.

3Stacked objects and bathroom scales

Worked example 3: you place the 0.50 kg book on a table and a 1.3 kg rock on top of the book. What is the normal force of the table on the book?

Treat the book and rock as one object: m = 0.50 + 1.3 = 1.8 kg, at rest. The table supports both.
Step 1. FBD of the book. Fn from the table (up); down: Fg of the book and the push of the rock on the book (the rock’s weight).
Step 2. At rest: Fn,table = (mbook + mrock)g = 1.8 × 9.8 = 17.6 N.
Step 3. Does the table push on the rock? No. The table is not in contact with the rock. The normal force on the rock comes from the book.
A normal force needs direct contact between two surfaces.

Worked example 4: a 40 kg person stands on a bathroom scale. What does the scale read?

The scale reading is the normal force on the person. a = 0.
Step 1. Fn = Fg = mg.
Step 2. Fn = 40 × 9.8 = 392 N.
Step 3. Note. Scales are labelled in kg, but they really measure the normal force and divide by 9.8.

4When other forces push or pull at an angle

A push or pull that has a vertical part changes the normal force. Only the vertical component counts for the vertical equation.

  • Pull up at an angle: the vertical component helps hold the object up, so Fn = mg − F sinθ. The normal force is smaller.
  • Push down at an angle: Fn = mg + F sinθ. The normal force is bigger.
  • Horizontal push or pull: no vertical part, so Fn = mg.
  • Block pressed against a wall: the wall’s Fn is horizontal and equal to the applied push (Fn = Fapp) if the block does not move sideways. Friction up the wall balances gravity (Ff = mg).

Worked example 5: a 5.0 kg box sits on the floor. A 10 N force pulls it up and to the right at 30° above the horizontal. Find the normal force.

Given: m = 5.0 kg, F = 10 N, θ = 30°. Up is positive; the box does not move vertically.
Step 1. Vertical part of the pull. Fy = 10 sin 30° = 5.0 N [up].
Step 2. Net force. Fn + Fy − Fg = 0.
Step 3. Solve. Fn = 49 − 5.0 = 44 N.
Step 4. Sense check. Pulling up takes some load off the floor, so Fn is smaller than 49 N. ✓

Worked example 6: the same box, but the 10 N force pushes down and to the right at 30° below the horizontal.

Now the vertical component points down.
Step 1. Fy = 5.0 N [down].
Step 2. Fn − Fg − Fy = 0, so Fn = 49 + 5.0 = 54 N.

5Knowledge check

6Hands-on: the bathroom scale

Weigh yourself while moving
  1. Stand on a bathroom scale. Note the reading.
  2. Bend your knees quickly and watch the reading as you speed up downward. Then stand up quickly.
  3. Hold a heavy backpack in your hands and push down on a table while standing on the scale, then pull up on the table.
Show a model answer

My weight (Fg) did not change. When I started bending down, my acceleration was down, so the net force was down and Fn < Fg: the reading dropped. When I started to stand up, my acceleration was up, so Fn > Fg: the reading rose. At rest, a = 0 and Fn = Fg.
