Velocity-time graphs: speed-ups, slow-downs and distance

Reading velocity against time, finding acceleration from the slope, and finding displacement from the area. About 60 minutes, plus a walk-and-time activity.

1The speedometer graph

Imagine a sprinter with a speedometer. Every second, someone writes down her velocity. She builds up speed for 4 seconds, holds a steady pace for 4 seconds, then slows to a stop over the last 2 seconds.

A velocity-time graph puts the velocity up the side and time along the bottom. It shows how the velocity changes, not where she is.

2How to read the shape

What the line doesWhat the motion is
Flat line above the axisConstant velocity (not zero), moving forward
Flat line right on the axis (v = 0)At rest
Flat line below the axisConstant velocity, moving backward
Straight line sloping upVelocity increasing at a steady rate: constant acceleration
Straight line sloping downVelocity decreasing at a steady rate
Line crossing the axisThe object stops for an instant and turns around
Watch out

A flat line on a velocity-time graph does not mean the object has stopped. At the height of 8 m/s, a flat line means a steady 8 m/s. The object is only at rest when the line sits on the axis. This is the most common mix-up with position-time graphs, where a flat line does mean at rest.

Quick rule: check the label on the vertical axis first. Every shape means something different depending on whether it says position or velocity.

3Slope is acceleration

In plain words

Acceleration is how quickly velocity changes. The slope of a velocity-time line is the change in velocity divided by the change in time, so the slope is the acceleration.

a = Δv ÷ Δt = (v2 − v1) ÷ (t2 − t1)

The unit is (m/s) ÷ s = m/s². Read it as “metres per second, every second.” An acceleration of 2.0 m/s² means the velocity goes up by 2.0 m/s each second.

Worked example 1: find the acceleration in stage A of the sprinter’s graph (0 s to 4.0 s).

Given: v = 0 at t = 0 s and v = 8.0 m/s at t = 4.0 s. Find: a.
Step 1. Rise. Δv = 8.0 − 0 = 8.0 m/s.
Step 2. Run. Δt = 4.0 − 0 = 4.0 s.
Step 3. Divide. a = 8.0 ÷ 4.0 = 2.0 m/s² (positive: speeding up in the positive direction).
Step 4. Sense check. 2.0 m/s² means +2 m/s each second: 2, 4, 6, 8 m/s after 1, 2, 3, 4 s. That matches the table. ✓

Worked example 2: stage B (4.0 s to 8.0 s) and stage C (8.0 s to 10.0 s).

Stage B: v stays at 8.0 m/s. Stage C: v goes from 8.0 m/s to 0 in 2.0 s.
Stage B. Δv = 8.0 − 8.0 = 0, so a = 0 ÷ 4.0 = 0 m/s². A flat line has zero slope: no acceleration.
Stage C. Δv = 0 − 8.0 = −8.0 m/s and Δt = 10.0 − 8.0 = 2.0 s, so a = −8.0 ÷ 2.0 = −4.0 m/s².
The negative sign here means the velocity is decreasing. She is still going forward but slowing down.
Sense check. Stage C is steeper than stage A (she stops faster than she started), so the size of its acceleration (4.0) is bigger than A’s (2.0). ✓

4Area is displacement

In plain words

Velocity × time = displacement. On a velocity-time graph, the area between the line and the time axis is the displacement. Rectangles and triangles are all you need.

ShapeAreaWhen you see it
Rectanglev × ΔtConstant velocity (flat line)
Triangle½ × base × heightA line starting at zero or ending at zero
Trapezoidrectangle + triangle (add the two)A line that rises, then flattens

Area above the time axis is positive displacement (forward). Area below the axis is negative displacement (backward).

Worked example 3: find the sprinter’s total displacement from the graph in part 1.

Three shapes: triangle A (0 to 4 s), rectangle B (4 to 8 s), triangle C (8 to 10 s). Height = 8.0 m/s.
Step 1. Triangle A. ½ × 4.0 s × 8.0 m/s = 16 m.
Step 2. Rectangle B. 4.0 s × 8.0 m/s = 32 m.
Step 3. Triangle C. ½ × 2.0 s × 8.0 m/s = 8.0 m.
Step 4. Add. 16 + 32 + 8.0 = 56 m.
Step 5. Average velocity. 56 m ÷ 10 s = 5.6 m/s.
Units check for the area: (m/s) × s = m. The area really is a length.

Now a trickier one, where the line goes below the axis. The green area is forward, the red area is backward.

The line crosses zero at 3 s. Each coloured area is labelled with its displacement.

Worked example 4: find the displacement and the distance for the graph above.

Areas: +8 m (rectangle), +2 m (triangle above), −2 m (triangle below), −8 m (rectangle below).
Step 1. Displacement adds with signs. 8 + 2 − 2 − 8 = 0 m. She finishes where she started.
Step 2. Distance adds sizes only. 8 + 2 + 2 + 8 = 20 m.
This is Lesson 1 again: displacement 0, distance 20. The graph shows both at once.
Step 3. Sense check. The red and green areas are the same size, so they cancel. ✓

6In the real world, and hands-on

Cars and elevators. An elevator starts slowly, cruises at a steady speed, then slows to a stop. Its velocity-time graph is the same shape as the sprinter’s: a ramp, a flat top, a ramp down. That is why you feel pushed into the floor at the start and lighter at the end.

A winger. A winger who sprints, jogs back, then sprints again draws a velocity-time graph that goes up, dips and goes up again. Fitness trackers use velocity data like this.

Speed up and time it
  1. Lay out 6 cones in a line, 2 m apart, at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 m.
  2. A partner stands at the 10 m end with a stopwatch. Start from rest at the first cone and speed up steadily all the way to the last cone. The partner calls out the time at each cone.
  3. Type the six times. Then work out your velocity for each 2 m section (2.0 m ÷ the time for that section).
  4. On paper, plot velocity (up) against time (across), using the middle of each time section. Do the points form a line sloping up? What is its slope?

Rate yourself honestly. These ratings are for planning the next step, not for marks.
