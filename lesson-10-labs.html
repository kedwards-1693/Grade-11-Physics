Lab skills: the two Vernier cart labs

How to get clean data, read the graphs, and write the discussion answers for the Uniform Motion and Accelerated Motion labs. About 60 minutes.

Before you start

Have these ready:

  • Your lab handouts and data package from Google Classroom
  • Calculator
  • Pencil and paper

This lesson does not replace doing the labs. It helps you understand what the graphs should look like, so you can spot a problem and explain your results.

1Uniform motion lab: what to expect

The cart moves at a steady speed (trial 1 slow, trial 2 fast, trial 3 in reverse). You compare two ways to find the velocity.

GraphWhat it should look likeHow you get velocity
Position-time (d–t)A straight sloped lineThe slope of the line
Velocity-time (v–t)A flat horizontal lineThe mean (average) value
  • A faster cart gives a steeper d–t line and a higher flat v–t line.
  • The reverse trial has a negative slope and a v–t line below zero. The sign tells you the direction.
  • The area under the v–t line is the displacement.
Why the two methods may differ a little

Real carts have friction, so they slow down a tiny bit. The line is almost straight, not perfectly straight. The two velocities should agree closely, and the small difference is explained by friction and measurement limits.

2Accelerated motion lab: what to expect

The cart rolls down a ramp (trial 1 gentle, trial 2 steeper, trial 3 launched up the ramp, which rolls up and comes back).

GraphShapeWhat to read from it
d–tA curve (a parabola)Fit d = At² + Bt + C. The acceleration is a = 2A.
v–tA straight sloped lineThe slope is the acceleration
a–tA flat horizontal lineThe mean is the acceleration
A note on the handout

The background text in the lab says “A = 2a”. From the equation d = vit + ½at², the number in front of t² is half the acceleration, so A = a ÷ 2 and therefore a = 2A. Use a = 2A in your calculations. If you are unsure, ask your teacher.

The up-and-back trial (trial 3): the v–t line is still one straight line sloping down, crossing zero at the highest point. At that instant the cart is stopped (v = 0) but the acceleration is not zero, because the velocity is still changing. The a–t graph is flat the whole time, and the sign is the same going up and coming down.

3Percent difference

The labs ask you to compare two values for the same quantity (for example, the velocity from the slope and the velocity from the mean). The percent difference tells you how close they are.

Formula

% difference = |A − B| ÷ [(A + B) ÷ 2] × 100

Check your lab package for the exact formula your teacher wants, and use that one.

Worked example: the slope gives 0.42 m/s and the mean of the v–t graph gives 0.40 m/s. Find the percent difference.

A = 0.42 m/s, B = 0.40 m/s.
Step 1. |A − B| = 0.02.
Step 2. Average = (0.42 + 0.40) ÷ 2 = 0.41.
Step 3. 0.02 ÷ 0.41 × 100 = 4.9%.
Step 4. Meaning. The two methods agree within about 5%, which is good for a real cart. Small differences come from friction and sensor limits, not from a mistake.

5Write your discussion answers

Strong lab answers have three parts: say what you found, use a number from your data, and explain why with physics. Practise on this one, then open the model answer.

Question: In the accelerated motion lab, a student says, “At the top of the cart’s path in trial 3, the velocity is zero, so the acceleration must also be zero.” Is the student correct? Explain.

Show a model answer

The student is not correct. At the top the velocity is momentarily zero, but the velocity is still changing: it goes from positive (moving up) to negative (moving down). In my v–t graph the line keeps the same slope as it crosses zero, and the a–t graph stays flat at about −(the same value) the whole time. So the acceleration is not zero. Gravity along the ramp keeps pulling the cart back down, even at the instant the cart stops.

Other lab claims to be ready for:

  • “Push the cart harder and it will have a larger acceleration.” After the push ends, the acceleration comes from gravity along the ramp, not from the push. A harder push changes the starting velocity, not the acceleration.
  • “A cart with double the mass will have double the acceleration.” Not correct. Ignoring friction, both have the same acceleration, because the pull of gravity along the ramp is also doubled, and the two effects cancel (like a heavy and a light object in free fall).
  • Ideal versus real: an ideal cart would have a perfectly flat a–t line. A real cart has some friction, so the line wobbles or drifts slightly, and the up trial has a slightly bigger acceleration than the down trial.

6Lab report checklist and Google Classroom

  • Graphs have a title, axis labels with units, and (for fitted graphs) the equation shown.
  • Each calculation shows the formula, the substitution with units, and the answer with the right number of digits.
  • Percent differences are calculated for each trial and commented on.
  • Discussion answers follow the pattern: claim, number from my data, physics reason.
  • I used a = 2A for the quadratic fit.

In Google Classroom, open Uniform Motion Lab and Accelerated Motion Lab, and compare each of your graphs with the shapes in parts 1 and 2. If a graph looks different, ask yourself what might have happened (cart tilted, sensor pointing away, push too hard, ramp not steady).

10How did that go?

Rate yourself honestly. These ratings are for planning the next step, not for marks.

  • I can predict the d–t, v–t and a–t shapes for constant velocity and constant acceleration.
  • I can get velocity from a slope and from a mean, and compare them with a percent difference.
  • I can get acceleration from a quadratic fit (a = 2A), a v–t slope, and an a–t mean.
  • I can explain why v = 0 does not mean a = 0.
  • I can explain real-cart differences (friction) and answer the push-harder and double-mass claims.
