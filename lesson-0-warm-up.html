1Why bother?

In 1999, NASA lost a spacecraft at Mars. One team’s software worked in imperial units and another team’s expected metric. The mismatch went unnoticed. A number with the wrong unit is not a small mistake. It is a wrong answer.

On physics tests the same four things lose marks again and again: units, powers of ten, rounding and algebra. This warm-up fixes all four, so the rest of Kinematics is easier.

2Units and conversions

In plain words

Every measurement is a number and a unit. “12” means nothing. “12 m/s” means something. In this course you will mostly use metres (m), seconds (s) and kilograms (kg).

Prefixes change the size of the unit. These are on the back of your formula sheet:

PrefixSymbolMeaningExample

The method: multiply by a fraction that equals 1

Since 1000 m is the same length as 1 km, the fraction (1000 m ÷ 1 km) equals 1. Multiplying by it changes the unit, not the amount. Units cancel the same way numbers do.

2.4 km × (1000 m ÷ 1 km) = 2400 m

Try the speed converter. Watch which way the number moves.

Worked example: a free kick leaves the boot at 108 km/h. How fast is that in m/s?

Step 1. Write what you have and what you want.
108 km/h → ? m/s.
Two units change: km to m, and h to s.
Step 2. Set up one fraction for each change.
108 km/h × (1000 m ÷ 1 km) × (1 h ÷ 3600 s)
Put the unit you want to get rid of on the bottom of its fraction.
Step 3. Cancel the units.
km cancels with km, h cancels with h. You are left with m/s. ✓
Step 4. Calculate.
108 × 1000 ÷ 3600 = 30 m/s. The shortcut, 108 ÷ 3.6, gives the same.
Step 5. Sense check.
30 m/s means 30 metres every second, so the ball could cross a 100 m field in about 3.3 s. That is believable for a hard shot.

Times work the same way: 45 min × (60 s ÷ 1 min) = 2700 s.

Watch out

km/h → m/s makes the number smaller (divide by 3.6). m/s → km/h makes it bigger (multiply by 3.6). If your answer moves the wrong way, you flipped it. A sprinting player is about 10 m/s, which is about 36 km/h.

3Scientific notation

In plain words

Physics uses very big and very small numbers. Scientific notation writes them as a number from 1 up to (but not including) 10, times a power of ten. The power of ten tells you how far the decimal point moved.

  • Speed of light: 300 000 000 m/s = 3.00×108 m/s (the decimal moved 8 places left, so the exponent is +8).
  • A thin hair: 0.000 07 m = 7×10−5 m (the decimal moved 5 places right, so the exponent is −5).

The rule: move the decimal until there is one non-zero digit in front of it. Moved left, the exponent is positive (a big number). Moved right, it is negative (a small number).

More examples: 6 200 000 J = 6.2×106 J  and  0.000 045 m = 4.5×10−5 m. Prefixes are the same idea: 3.78 nm = 3.78×10−9 m.

Watch out

Use the EXP (or EE) key on your calculator: 3.00 EXP 8. If you type 3.00 × 10 EXP 8 you get a number ten times too big.

4Significant digits

In plain words

Significant digits show how precisely something was measured. A stopwatch reading of 8.2 s is less precise than 8.213 s. Your answer can never be more precise than your measurements, so you round to match.

Type any number below and see which digits count.

Which digits count

  1. Non-zero digits always count: 2.37 has 3.
  2. Zeros between non-zero digits count: 4.05 has 3.
  3. Zeros at the front never count: 0.0062 has 2.
  4. Zeros at the end count if there is a decimal point: 3.50 has 3, and 20.0 has 3.
  5. Zeros at the end of a whole number are unclear: 1500 could be 2, 3 or 4 digits. Scientific notation settles it: 1.5×103 has 2, 1.50×103 has 3.

How to round answers

  • Multiply or divide: keep as many digits as the measurement with the fewest. 12.6 m ÷ 2.0 s = 6.3 m/s (2 digits, because of the 2.0).
  • Add or subtract: keep as many decimal places as the measurement with the fewest. 12.34 m + 1.2 m = 13.54, which becomes 13.5 m (1 decimal place).
  • Exact numbers never limit you: counting numbers, and definitions like 1000 m in 1 km or 60 s in 1 min.
Watch out

Round once, at the very end. Rounding in the middle of a problem shifts the final digit. Keep the extra digits on your calculator and round only your final answer.

5Rearranging equations

In plain words

An equation is a balance. Whatever you do to one side, you do to the other side. To get a letter by itself, undo whatever is attached to it.

Check with units: d = v × t gives (m/s) × s = m. The seconds cancel and you are left with metres. If your units do not come out right, the rearranging went wrong.

The same moves work on any equation you will meet. For a = Δv ÷ Δt: Δv = a × Δt, and Δt = Δv ÷ a.

Watch out

Δ (delta) means “change in” and belongs to the letter next to it. Δt is one symbol, not Δ times t. Never cancel the deltas.

7Hands-on: time your own sprint

Try it with a ball and cones

Mark a 20.0 m straight line (goal line to a cone works). Have someone time you with a phone stopwatch while you run it flat out. Do three runs.

  1. Write each time with 2 significant digits (like 3.8 s). A person starting and stopping a stopwatch is off by about 0.2 s, so the hundredths are not real.
  2. Speed = distance ÷ time. Example: 20.0 m ÷ 3.8 s = 5.263… → 5.3 m/s (2 digits, because the time only has 2).
  3. Convert to km/h by multiplying by 3.6. Example: 5.263 × 3.6 = 18.95, so about 19 km/h.
  4. Compare with an elite player (about 10 m/s). Where do you land?

This is the first lab-style skill: take a measurement, calculate, convert, and round to the right digits.
