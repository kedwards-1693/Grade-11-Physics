Acceleration: how quickly velocity changes

Speeding up, slowing down and turning around, signs that finally make sense, and how acceleration shows up on graphs. About 60 minutes, plus a ramp activity.

Before you start

Have these ready:

  • Calculator, pencil and paper
  • The SPH3U Formula Sheet from Google Classroom
  • For the activity in part 7: a board or tray, a few books to tilt it, a small ball and a phone stopwatch

Done Lesson 3? You already found acceleration as a slope there. This lesson gives you the equation and the sign rules.

1Gas pedal, brake, and the post

In a car there are three controls that change how you move: the gas pedal, the brake and the steering wheel. Physics calls all three acceleration: any change in velocity. In this unit we stay on a straight line, so there are two cases:

  • the velocity grows (gas pedal): speeding up,
  • the velocity shrinks (brake): slowing down. If the velocity passes through zero and flips sign, the object turns around.

A goalkeeper catching a shot, a car stopping at a light, and a ball bouncing off the goalpost are all accelerations. The size of the acceleration says how quickly the velocity changes.

2The equation

In plain words

Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time it took. It says how much the velocity changes every second.

aav = Δv ÷ Δt = (vf − vi) ÷ Δt

PartWhat it means
vfFinal velocity (at the end of the time), with its sign
viInitial velocity (at the start), with its sign
ΔtTime that passed (in seconds, to match m/s)
m/s ÷ s = m/s²“Metres per second, every second.” 3.0 m/s² means +3.0 m/s each second.

The important part is the signs. Pick a positive direction first. Then:

VelocityAccelerationWhat happens
++Speeding up (forward)
+−Slowing down (while still going forward)
−−Speeding up (backward)
−+Slowing down (while still going backward)
In plain words

Same signs: speeding up. Opposite signs: slowing down. A negative acceleration does not mean “slowing down.” It only means the acceleration points in the negative direction.

Watch out

When an object reverses (like a ball bouncing back), the final velocity has the opposite sign from the initial velocity. Put the sign in before you subtract: vf − vi = (−10) − (+15) = −25, not 10 − 15.

3Worked examples

Worked example 1: a sprinter goes from rest to 9.0 m/s in 3.0 s. Find her average acceleration.

Given: vi = 0 (from rest); vf = 9.0 m/s; Δt = 3.0 s. Find: a.
Step 1. Equation. a = (vf − vi) ÷ Δt.
Step 2. Substitute. a = (9.0 − 0) ÷ 3.0.
Step 3. Solve. a = 3.0 m/s², positive: speeding up forward.
Step 4. Sense check. 3.0 m/s² means +3.0 m/s each second: 3.0, 6.0, 9.0 after 1, 2, 3 s. ✓

Worked example 2: a car moving at 25 m/s [E] brakes to a stop in 5.0 s. Find its acceleration. Take east as positive.

Given: vi = +25 m/s; vf = 0; Δt = 5.0 s.
Step 1. Substitute. a = (0 − 25) ÷ 5.0.
Step 2. Solve. a = −5.0 m/s². In words: 5.0 m/s² [W].
Opposite signs for v and a mean slowing down. The car is going east, and the acceleration points west.
Step 3. Sense check. Each second the velocity drops by 5.0 m/s: 25, 20, 15, 10, 5, 0. That takes 5 s. ✓

Worked example 3: a ball hits the goalpost at 15 m/s [E] and bounces back at 10 m/s [W]. The contact lasts 0.050 s. Find its average acceleration (east positive).

Given: vi = +15 m/s; vf = −10 m/s (west); Δt = 0.050 s.
Step 1. Signs first. Moving west, with east positive, means vf = −10 m/s.
Step 2. Substitute. a = (−10 − 15) ÷ 0.050 = −25 ÷ 0.050.
Step 3. Solve. a = −5.0×102 m/s², which is 5.0×102 m/s² [W].
That is more than 50 times the acceleration of gravity. A short contact time makes a big acceleration.
Step 4. Sense check. The velocity flipped from east to west, so the change is big (25 m/s) and the time is tiny. A large acceleration is right. ✓

Worked example 4: a cyclist speeds up from rest to 36 km/h in 4.0 s. Find her acceleration in m/s².

Given: vi = 0; vf = 36 km/h; Δt = 4.0 s.
Step 1. Match units. Time is in seconds, so convert the velocity: 36 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 10 m/s.
Step 2. Substitute. a = (10 − 0) ÷ 4.0.
Step 3. Solve. a = 2.5 m/s².
Step 4. Sense check. Gentle: the velocity grows by 2.5 m/s each second, which feels like a normal bike start. ✓

4Acceleration and position-time graphs

Here is one motion shown on three graphs: an object starting from rest and speeding up steadily at 2.0 m/s².

Left: position curves upward, getting steeper. Middle: velocity climbs along a straight line, and its slope is 2.0. Right: the acceleration graph is flat, because the acceleration is constant.

MotionPosition-timeVelocity-timeAcceleration-time
Constant velocitystraight line (any slope)flat lineflat on zero
Speeding up steadily (+)curve getting steeperstraight line sloping upflat above zero
Slowing down steadily (still going +)curve getting flatterstraight line sloping downflat below zero

Position-time graphs hide acceleration in the curvature. A straight position-time line means a = 0. A curved one means the velocity is changing, so there is an acceleration. Curving up (“smile” shape) with the object moving forward means speeding up. Curving the other way (“frown” shape) means slowing down.

Watch out

The curve of a position-time graph shows that there is acceleration, but you cannot read its size directly. To get the number, find the velocity from tangent lines at two times and use a = Δv ÷ Δt.

6In the real world

Gravity. A dropped ball speeds up by 9.8 m/s every second. So its acceleration is 9.8 m/s², which is where the unit comes from.

Car safety. A seatbelt and an air bag make the stopping time longer. With the same change in velocity, a longer Δt makes a smaller acceleration, so less force on your body.

Soccer. A goalkeeper who pulls her hands back as she catches a fast shot lengthens Δt, so the ball’s acceleration, and the sting in her hands, is smaller.

7Hands-on: build an acceleration

The ramp
  1. Prop one end of a board or tray on a few books to make a gentle ramp, about 1.5 m of travel.
  2. Mark a start line and a finish line. Measure the distance between them (Δd).
  3. Release a small ball from rest at the start line. A partner times it to the finish line. Do 3 runs and use the middle time.
  4. Because it starts from rest and speeds up steadily, the average velocity is halfway between 0 and the final velocity. So vf = 2 × vav.
  5. Then a = vf ÷ Δt. Fill in the boxes. Then raise the ramp and try again. What changes?
