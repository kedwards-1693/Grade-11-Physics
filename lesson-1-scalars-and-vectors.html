1The winger’s run

A coach tracks a winger with GPS. She runs 30 m towards the opponent’s goal, then turns and runs 18 m back to help defend. The GPS says she ran 48 m. The coach says she ended up 12 m ahead of where she started.

They are both right. They are answering different questions. One is about how much ground she covered. The other is about where she ended up. Physics keeps those two ideas apart, and that is what this lesson is about.

2Position, distance and displacement

In plain words

A scalar has a size only. A vector has a size and a direction.

Position is where you are, measured from a starting point, with a direction. (“The corner flag is 50 m [NE] of the goal.”) Distance is how much ground you covered along the path, like an odometer reading. Displacement is your change in position: a straight arrow from where you started to where you finished, however twisty the path.

QuantitySymbolScalar or vector?Direction?
PositiondVectorYes
DisplacementΔdVectorYes
DistancedScalarNo

The arrow over the letter means “this one has a direction.” Displacement is found like this:

Δd = df − di

Final position minus initial position. The Δ means “change in,” and f and i mean final and initial. Play with the run below. Change the numbers and watch how the distance and the displacement behave.

Watch out

Distance can never be smaller than the size of the displacement. If you run out and back to the same spot, the distance is large but the displacement is zero. A full lap of a track is the classic case.

3Speed and velocity

In plain words

Speed is how fast you are going: the odometer divided by the clock. Velocity is how fast and which way: it uses displacement, not distance.

In wordsEquationUnitsType
average speed = distance ÷ timevav = d ÷ Δtm/sScalar
average velocity = displacement ÷ timevav = Δd ÷ Δtm/s [direction]Vector

Δt is the time that passed: final time minus initial time.

Worked example 1: the winger runs 30 m [E], then 18 m [W], in 8.0 s. Find her distance, displacement, average speed and average velocity.

Given: 30 m [E] then 18 m [W]; Δt = 8.0 s. Take east as positive.
Step 1. Sketch it. Draw a line. Start at 0, arrow 30 m to the east, then 18 m back west.
A quick sketch stops sign mistakes before they start.
Step 2. Distance adds up every bit of path, no direction: 30 + 18 = 48 m.
Step 3. Displacement is final minus initial. She ends at +30 − 18 = +12, and started at 0. Δd = 12 − 0 = 12 m [E].
Step 4. Average speed uses distance: 48 m ÷ 8.0 s = 6.0 m/s.
Step 5. Average velocity uses displacement: 12 m [E] ÷ 8.0 s = 1.5 m/s [E].
Two numbers for the same run, and both are correct. Speed answers “how busy was she?” Velocity answers “how fast did her position change?”
Step 6. Sense check. The speed is bigger than the velocity, which is always true when there is a turn-around. Units are m/s. Digits: 8.0 has 2, so each answer has 2. ✓

Worked example 2: a jogger holds 2.5 m/s for 12 minutes. How far does she go?

Given: v = 2.5 m/s; Δt = 12 min. Find: d.
Step 1. Match the units. The speed is in m/s, so the time must be in seconds: 12 min × 60 = 720 s.
Step 2. Pick the equation. We want distance, so rearrange v = d ÷ Δt to d = v × Δt.
Step 3. Calculate. d = 2.5 m/s × 720 s = 1800 m.
Step 4. Round. 2.5 has 2 digits and 12 has 2, so the answer gets 2: 1.8×103 m (or 1.8 km). The unit check: (m/s) × s = m. ✓

4Directions and signs

Directions are written in square brackets after the number: [N] north, [S] south, [E] east, [W] west, [U] up, [D] down, [L] left, [R] right, [f] forward, [b] backward. Angles like [N 30° W] come later, in the lesson on 2-D vectors.

In plain words

In a straight-line problem, pick one direction to be positive. The opposite direction is then negative. A negative answer is not “wrong.” It just means “the other way.”

The same position can be written two ways: 15 m [W] = −15 m [E]. In the first version west is the positive direction. In the second, east is.

Worked example 3: a ball starts 8 m [E] of the penalty spot and is kicked to a spot 5 m [W] of the penalty spot. Find its displacement.

Take east as positive. Initial position: +8 m. Final position: −5 m.
Step 1. Sketch it. Draw the spot as 0. Put the start at +8 on the east side and the finish at −5 on the west side.
Step 2. Use the equation. Δd = df − di = (−5) − (+8) = −13 m.
Step 3. Say what the sign means. East was positive, so −13 m means 13 m to the west: 13 m [W].
A common slip is 8 − 5 = 3. That would only work if both positions were on the same side of the spot.
Step 4. Sense check. The ball crossed the spot, so it travelled 8 m to reach it and then 5 m beyond. 8 + 5 = 13. ✓
Watch out

Always subtract final minus initial, and keep the signs. When the start and the finish are on opposite sides of the zero point, the sizes add.

6In the real world, and hands-on

Maps and GPS. Your phone says the drive to a friend’s house is 5.2 km, but the straight line on the map is about 3.1 km. The 5.2 km is a distance (the path). The 3.1 km, with its direction, is the displacement.

Skating in Ottawa. The Rideau Canal Skateway is roughly 7.8 km long. Skate from one end to the other and back, and you have covered about 15.6 km, yet your displacement is zero and so is your average velocity. Your fitness watch counts distance. A map only shows your displacement.

Try it with a ball and cones: walk it and measure it
  1. Put a cone at a start spot. Place a second cone 10 m away to the east (or any direction you pick) and a third cone 4 m back toward the start.
  2. Start the stopwatch, walk from the start to the far cone, then back to the third cone, and stop the watch.
  3. Type your measurements for the first walk in the boxes below.
  4. Draw it as an arrow diagram on paper. Which one is bigger, your speed or the size of your velocity? Why? Type your answer below.
  5. Try it again in a way that makes the displacement zero. Type what you measured in the second walk.
