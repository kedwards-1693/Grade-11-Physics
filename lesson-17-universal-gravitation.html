Universal gravitation

Gravity between any two masses, why g is 9.8 m/s² on Earth, and how weight changes on other planets and far from Earth. About 90 minutes.

Before you start

Have these ready:

  • Calculator in scientific notation mode (the EE or ×10x key)
  • Pencil and paper
  • Your Gravity slides and Gravity Problems sheet from Google Classroom

Lesson 0 (scientific notation) helps here: the numbers are very big or very small. Enter 6.67 × 10−11 as 6.67 EE −11 on your calculator, not as 6.67 × 10 × −11.

1Gravity pulls everywhere, between everything

  • Universal means it works everywhere in the universe. There is gravity in space.
  • Every mass pulls on every other mass. It is always a pull (attraction), along the line joining the two masses.
  • By the third law the two forces are a pair: the Earth pulls on the apple and the apple pulls on the Earth, equally.
  • Yes, you and the person next to you pull on each other. The force is just far too small to notice (see example 1).

2The equation

Newton’s law of universal gravitation

Fg = G m1m2 ÷ r²

  • Fg: force of gravity on each mass (N)
  • G = 6.67 × 10−11 N·m²/kg² (universal gravitational constant)
  • m1, m2: the two masses (kg)
  • r: distance between the centres of the masses (m)
  • Both masses matter. Double one mass and the force doubles.
  • Distance is squared in the denominator (an inverse-square law). Double the distance and the force becomes one quarter. Triple it and the force is one ninth.
  • G is tiny, so the force is only large when at least one mass is huge (a planet or moon).

The inverse-square law: moving farther from the centre makes the force fall off quickly.

Watch out

The distance r is measured from the centre of the planet, not from its surface. Standing on Earth’s surface, r is Earth’s radius (6.38 × 106 m). A satellite 400 km up has r = 6.38 × 106 + 4.0 × 105 m. And square r after you have found it.

3Worked examples

Worked example 1: you and a friend each have a mass of 60 kg and sit 20 cm apart. How strong is the pull between you?

Given: m1 = m2 = 60 kg, r = 0.20 m.
Step 1. Fg = (6.67 × 10−11)(60)(60) ÷ 0.20².
Step 2. Top: 6.67 × 10−11 × 3600 = 2.40 × 10−7. Divide by 0.040: 6.0 × 10−6 N [toward your friend].
Step 3. Sense check. About a millionth of a newton, the weight of a tiny speck of dust. You can’t feel it.
To get a bigger force you need a bigger mass, or a smaller distance.

Worked example 2: a 55 kg person stands on the Moon (mass 7.4 × 1022 kg, radius 1.74 × 106 m). What is the force of gravity on the person?

Given: m1 = 55 kg, m2 = 7.4 × 1022 kg, r = 1.74 × 106 m (the Moon’s radius, since the person is on the surface).
Step 1. Fg = (6.67 × 10−11)(55)(7.4 × 1022) ÷ (1.74 × 106)².
Step 2. Top: 2.71 × 1014. Bottom: 3.03 × 1012. Fg = 90 N toward the Moon’s centre.
Step 3. This is the person’s weight on the Moon. On Earth it would be 55 × 9.8 = 539 N, about six times more.
(Your slide shows 88 N because it rounded the Moon’s numbers a little. Rounding differences are fine.)

Worked example 3: find g on the surface of Earth (M = 5.98 × 1024 kg, r = 6.38 × 106 m).

The weight of an object is Fg = mg. Set that equal to G M m ÷ r². The object’s mass m cancels!
Step 1. The field-strength equation. g = GM ÷ r². It depends on the planet only, not on the object.
Step 2. g = (6.67 × 10−11)(5.98 × 1024) ÷ (6.38 × 106)² = 9.8 m/s² [down].
Step 3. Why g is the same for a feather and a hammer in a vacuum. The object’s mass cancels, so every object falls with the same acceleration.
g is also the gravitational field strength, measured in N/kg. 9.8 m/s² = 9.8 N/kg.

Worked example 4: an object has a weight of 500 N on Earth’s surface. Use ratios to find its weight at (a) half the distance from the centre, (b) 1/8 the distance, (c) 4 times the distance.

Weight is proportional to 1 ÷ r². If the distance is multiplied by k, the force is multiplied by 1 ÷ k².
(a) k = 1/2, so the force is multiplied by 1 ÷ (1/2)² = 4: 500 × 4 = 2.0 × 10³ N.
(b) k = 1/8, so multiply by 64: 500 × 64 = 3.2 × 10⁴ N.
(c) k = 4, so multiply by 1/16: 500 ÷ 16 = 31.25, or 31 N.
Sense check. Closer means a stronger pull, and farther means a weaker pull. ✓

Worked example 5: a 1000 kg satellite orbits at 42 400 km from the Earth’s centre. Find the force of gravity on it and the gravitational field strength there.

Given: m = 1000 kg, M = 5.98 × 1024 kg, r = 4.24 × 107 m.
Step 1. Force. Fg = (6.67 × 10−11)(5.98 × 1024)(1000) ÷ (4.24 × 107)² = 222 N.
Step 2. Field strength. g = Fg ÷ m = 222 ÷ 1000 = 0.222 N/kg.
Step 3. Sense check. Far from Earth the pull is much weaker than 9.8 N/kg. ✓

4Weightlessness and why it matters

  • Apparent weightlessness. In free fall, the only force is gravity, so there is no normal force (Fn = 0). A scale would read zero, so you feel weightless even though gravity is still pulling you. Astronauts in orbit are in free fall around the Earth: gravity is still about 90% of its surface value there.
  • Why animals need skeletons. On land, bones and muscles must hold you up against gravity all the time. Astronauts in weightless conditions for months lose bone and muscle strength, because the body is not working against gravity.
  • Do athletes care about g? Yes, slightly. g is a little smaller at high elevation (you are farther from Earth’s centre), so a throw or a jump can go a little farther at altitude. That is one reason records are noted for the elevation of the stadium.

6Hands-on: your weight on other worlds

Use g = GM ÷ r² and F = mg

Use your own mass in kilograms (ask a parent, or use the bathroom scale reading). The planet values are:

BodyMass (kg)Radius (m)
Moon7.4 × 10221.74 × 106
Mars6.42 × 10233.39 × 106
Jupiter1.90 × 10277.15 × 107

Check yourself: the field strengths are about 1.63 N/kg (Moon), 3.73 N/kg (Mars) and 24.8 N/kg (Jupiter). Your mass does not change anywhere. Only your weight does.

Show a model answer

My mass stays the same (it is how much matter I have), but my weight is bigger on Jupiter than on Earth because Jupiter’s gravitational field strength (24.8 N/kg) is bigger than Earth’s (9.8 N/kg). Weight = mg changes with g, not with mass.
