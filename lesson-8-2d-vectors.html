2-D vectors: directions at an angle, components and adding

Direction notation with angles, splitting a vector into components, and adding vectors in two dimensions. About 75 minutes, plus a walk-and-measure activity.

Before you start

Have these ready:

  • Calculator in degree mode (check it now: sin 30 should give 0.5)
  • Pencil, paper and a ruler
  • The SPH3U Formula Sheet from Google Classroom
  • For the activity in part 6: a tape measure or some chalk, and a partner

Done Lesson 1? You added displacements along one line there. Now we add them when they point in different directions.

1Why a straight line is not enough

A midfielder passes the ball 20 m east to a teammate, who turns and passes it 15 m north. The ball ended up 25 m from where it started, not 35 m. The path had a corner, so adding the sizes gives the wrong answer.

Vectors that are not on the same line need a different way to add them. There are two:

  • Draw them tip to tail and measure or calculate the arrow from the start to the finish.
  • Split each vector into an east part and a north part (its components), add the parts, then put the total back together.

2Direction notation with angles

In plain words

Square brackets give the direction. For an angle, say which direction you start facing, and then which way you turn from there.

WrittenRead it as
[N 30° E]Start facing north, turn 30° towards east
[E 60° N]Start facing east, turn 60° towards north (the same direction as [N 30° E])
[W 20° S]Start facing west, turn 20° towards south

Every 2-D direction can be written two ways, and the two angles add to 90°. [N 30° E] and [E 60° N] point the same way. For calculations, it is usually easiest to write the angle from the east-west line (the horizontal), which makes the formulas below simple.

Watch out

The angle is always measured from the first direction named, towards the second. [E 35° N] is only a little north of east. [N 35° E] is mostly north. Read the first letter first.

3Adding perpendicular vectors

When the two vectors are at right angles (east and north, for example), tip-to-tail gives a right triangle. The resultant is the hypotenuse.

20 m [E] then 15 m [N]. The dashed arrow is the resultant: the total displacement.

R = √(a² + b²)     θ = tan−1(b ÷ a)

Here a is the side the angle is next to (adjacent), b is the opposite side. θ is measured from the side a.

Worked example 1: the midfielder’s pass goes 20 m [E], then 15 m [N]. Find the total displacement.

Sketch: a right triangle with a = 20 m (east) and b = 15 m (north). Find: size and direction of the resultant.
Step 1. Size. R = √(20² + 15²) = √(400 + 225) = √625 = 25 m.
Step 2. Angle from east. θ = tan−1(15 ÷ 20) = tan−1(0.75) = 36.9°, which is 37° (2 digits).
The side next to the angle is the east side (20 m). The opposite side is the north side (15 m).
Step 3. Write the direction. Start at east, turn 37° towards north: [E 37° N]. Equivalent: [N 53° E].
Step 4. Sense check. The resultant (25 m) is shorter than 20 + 15 = 35 m but longer than either part. The direction is between east and north, closer to east, because the east part is bigger. ✓
Watch out

This shortcut (Pythagoras and tan−1) works only when the two vectors are at right angles. For other angles, use components (part 4).

4Components: splitting a vector

In plain words

Any vector at an angle can be replaced by two perpendicular vectors that do the same job: a horizontal component and a vertical component. If the angle θ is measured from the horizontal:

vx = v cos θ     vy = v sin θ

A quick check: when θ is small (nearly flat), the horizontal part should be almost the whole vector, and cos 0° = 1. When θ is large (nearly upright), the vertical part is almost the whole vector, and sin 90° = 1.

A kick of 22 m/s at 35° above the ground, split into its two components.

Worked example 2: a ball is kicked at 22 m/s [E 35° N]. Find its horizontal and vertical velocity components.

Given: v = 22 m/s; θ = 35° from east (the horizontal). Find: vx and vy.
Step 1. Horizontal. vx = v cos θ = 22 cos 35° = 22 × 0.819 = 18.0, so 18 m/s [E].
Step 2. Vertical. vy = v sin θ = 22 sin 35° = 22 × 0.574 = 12.6, so 13 m/s [N] (up).
Step 3. Sense check. Put them back together: √(18.0² + 12.6²) = √(324 + 159) = 22. ✓
The horizontal part is bigger because the angle is less than 45°.

Adding non-perpendicular vectors

  1. Split every vector into east/west and north/south components. Use + for east and north, − for west and south.
  2. Add all the east-west parts. Add all the north-south parts. These are the components of the resultant.
  3. Use Pythagoras and tan−1 on the two totals to find the size and direction.

Worked example 3: a player runs 45 m [E], then 30 m [N 40° E]. Find her total displacement.

Given: Δd1 = 45 m [E]; Δd2 = 30 m [N 40° E]. Angle for the second one is from north.
Step 1. Components of the first. East: +45 m. North: 0.
Step 2. Components of the second. The angle (40°) is from north, so the north part uses cos: 30 cos 40° = 23.0 m [N], and the east part uses sin: 30 sin 40° = 19.3 m [E].
The side next to the angle uses cos, and the side opposite the angle uses sin. Which side is “next to” depends on where the angle is measured from.
Step 3. Add. East total = 45 + 19.3 = 64.3 m. North total = 0 + 23.0 = 23.0 m.
Step 4. Size. R = √(64.3² + 23.0²) = √(4134 + 529) = 68.3, so 68 m.
Step 5. Direction. θ = tan−1(23.0 ÷ 64.3) = 19.7°, so [E 20° N].
Step 6. Sense check. 68 m is less than 45 + 30 = 75 m, and the direction is mostly east with a little north. ✓

6In the real world, and hands-on

Navigation. A pilot flying 200 km/h [N] in a wind of 40 km/h [E] ends up going faster than 200 and slightly east of north. That is a vector sum. The ground speed is √(200² + 40²) = 204 km/h.

Soccer. A pass that goes diagonally across the pitch is a sum of an across part and a forward part. Players think this way without noticing.

Walk it and measure it
  1. Mark a start point on the ground with chalk or a cone.
  2. Walk east (any direction you choose as east) a measured distance, such as 8.0 m. Turn right angle, and walk north a different distance, such as 6.0 m.
  3. Use a tape measure to measure the straight line from the start to where you ended up.
  4. Calculate the same distance with Pythagoras. Do they agree?
