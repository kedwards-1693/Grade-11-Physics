Free body diagrams

The one picture that shows every force on an object. About 75 minutes, with a typed practice table.

Before you start

1What is a free body diagram?

A free body diagram (FBD) shows the object as a dot or small box and draws an arrow for every force acting on it. It helps you find the net force and decide whether the object accelerates.

  1. Model the object as a point or small box.
  2. Draw each external force as an arrow starting at the point. The direction is the direction of the force. Lengths are roughly in proportion.
  3. Ask: “At this moment, what is pushing or pulling on the object?” Look for contact forces and gravity.
  4. Add a separate squiggly arrow for the acceleration (a⃗), drawn away from the object, so it is not mistaken for a force.
  5. Choose a coordinate system and sign convention (for example, right and up are positive).

2The forces and their symbols

ForceSymbolWhat it is
Gravity (weight)FgEarth pulling the object down
NormalFnA surface pushing on the object, perpendicular to the surface
TensionFtA rope or string pulling
AppliedFaA push or pull from a person
FrictionFfBetween sliding (or trying to slide) surfaces, opposite to the motion
Air resistanceFairAir pushing against the motion
Two rules from the worksheet

The air resistance rule: assume there is no air resistance unless the problem says so or it would not make sense.

Force sign convention: in a scalar equation, the force symbols are all positive sizes. Show direction by adding or subtracting. For example: Fnet = Fn − Fg.

Two things are not forces: velocity (the object’s motion) and the acceleration (the result of the net force). Never draw “the force of motion” or “the force that keeps it moving”.

3Worked examples

Each example gives the forces, a diagram, and the net-force equation.

Example A: a rock is falling (no air resistance).

Only one thing pulls on the rock: the Earth.
Forces: Fg only.
Net force (down positive): Fnet,y = Fg.
Acceleration: down. The rock speeds up.

Example B: a rock falls at a constant speed (terminal velocity).

Constant velocity means a = 0, so Fnet = 0.
Forces: Fg down, Fair up.
Net force: Fnet,y = Fair − Fg = 0, so Fair = Fg.
Question from the worksheet: “How can the rock be moving if the acceleration is zero?”
Zero acceleration means the velocity is not changing. It does not mean the velocity is zero. The rock moves at a steady speed.

Example C: a student pushes a cart. It leaves her hand and slows to a stop. Draw the FBD after release.

The cart is moving right. After release there is no hand force.
Forces: Fg down, Fn up, Ff to the left (opposite to motion).
x: Fnet,x = −Ff (left). y: Fnet,y = Fn − Fg = 0.
The hand force stops the instant the cart leaves her hand.
A force needs contact (or gravity). The hand is no longer touching the cart, so it cannot be pushing. There is no forward force. The cart slows because of friction.

Example D: a rock is tossed straight up. Draw the FBD while it is moving upward.

A student says, “There must be an upward force or it would not be moving up.”
Forces: only Fg (no air resistance, and the hand is no longer touching it).
Net force: Fnet,y = Fg downward.
Disagree with the student. The rock is moving up because of the velocity it was given, not because of an upward force. The net force points down, so it slows down.
Compare with Example A: the FBD is the same, even though one rock moves up and one moves down.
What an FBD cannot tell you: the direction of motion (velocity). It tells you the net force and so the acceleration, not whether the object is going up or down.

Example E: a rock is pulled along a rough surface by a horizontal string. It is slowing down.

Slowing down while moving right means the net force is left.
Forces: Ft right, Ff left, Fn up, Fg down.
x: Fnet,x = Ft − Ff and it is negative, so Ff > Ft.
y: Fn = Fg, because the string is horizontal and ay = 0. The horizontal forces are unequal, the vertical forces are equal.
