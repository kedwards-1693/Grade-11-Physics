Position-time graphs: the whole trip in one picture

Reading a graph, finding velocity from the slope, and predicting where someone will be. About 60 minutes, plus a walk-and-time activity.

Before reading on, try to answer from the graph: When was she standing still? When was she moving fastest? Did she come back?

Answers

Standing still: from 4 s to 6 s, where the line is flat (she is 12 m away and her position is not changing). Both jogging stretches are equally fast, but the line goes up on the way out and down on the way back. She did come back: at 10 s she is at 0 m again.

2How to read the shape

On a position-time graph, time is along the bottom (the x-axis) and position is up the side (the y-axis). Position depends on time, so time goes on the bottom.

What the line doesWhat the motion is
Flat (horizontal)Position is not changing: at rest, velocity is zero
Straight, sloping upSteady (constant) velocity in the positive direction
Straight, sloping up more steeplyA faster constant velocity
Straight, sloping downSteady velocity in the negative direction (back towards the zero point)
Curve getting steeperSpeeding up
Curve getting flatterSlowing down

Three players. Q has the steepest line, so Q is fastest. R slopes down, so R is moving back towards the goal.

Watch out

A position-time graph is not a picture of the path. If the line goes up, the player is not climbing a hill. She is running straight along the field, and her distance from the goal is growing.

3Slope is velocity

In plain words

The slope of a line tells you how steep it is: how much it rises for each step along. On a position-time graph, the rise is the change in position and the step along is the time taken. So the slope is velocity.

vav = Δd ÷ Δt = (d2 − d1) ÷ (t2 − t1)

PartWhat it means
Δd (the rise)How far the position changed: finish position minus start position (keep the sign)
Δt (the run)How much time passed: finish time minus start time
m ÷ s = m/sUnits check: position over time is velocity

For a straight line, any two points on the line give the same answer. Pick two points far apart that sit exactly on grid crossings. That makes the reading accurate.

Worked example 1: use the graph from part 1. Find her velocity from A (0 s, 0 m) to B (4.0 s, 12 m).

Given: point A (0 s, 0 m) and point B (4.0 s, 12 m). Find: velocity.
Step 1. Find the rise. Δd = 12 − 0 = 12 m.
Step 2. Find the run. Δt = 4.0 − 0 = 4.0 s.
Step 3. Divide. v = 12 m ÷ 4.0 s = 3.0 m/s, in the positive direction (away from the goal).
Step 4. Sense check. A jog of 3 m/s is about 11 km/h. That is believable. ✓

Worked example 2: find her velocity from C (6.0 s, 12 m) to D (10.0 s, 0 m).

Given: C (6.0 s, 12 m) and D (10.0 s, 0 m).
Step 1. Rise. Δd = 0 − 12 = −12 m. It is negative, because she is getting closer to the goal.
Step 2. Run. Δt = 10.0 − 6.0 = 4.0 s.
Step 3. Divide. v = −12 m ÷ 4.0 s = −3.0 m/s. That means 3.0 m/s towards the goal.
A negative slope is not a mistake. It means “moving in the negative direction.”
Step 4. Sense check. The line slopes down, so the velocity should be negative. It is, and the size matches the trip out. ✓

Worked example 3: find her average velocity AND her average speed for the whole 10 s.

Given: she starts at 0 m and finishes at 0 m. She jogs 12 m out and 12 m back.
Step 1. Average velocity uses displacement. Final position minus initial position is 0 − 0 = 0 m. So vav = 0 ÷ 10 = 0 m/s.
Step 2. Average speed uses distance. 12 + 12 = 24 m in 10 s, so the speed is 24 ÷ 10 = 2.4 m/s.
Step 3. Say why they differ. She finished where she started, so the displacement is zero even though she covered 24 m.
This is the same idea as the winger in Lesson 1. A graph just lets you see it.

4Using a straight line to predict

If the line is straight, the velocity is constant. Then you can work out where the player will be at any later time:

d = di + vΔt

In words: position now = where you started + (velocity × time passed). On the graph, di is where the line crosses the vertical axis.

Worked example 4: a player starts 5.0 m from the goal and jogs away at a steady 2.0 m/s. How far from the goal is she after 6.0 s?

Given: di = 5.0 m; v = 2.0 m/s (away from the goal, so positive); Δt = 6.0 s. Find: d.
Step 1. Find how far she moved. Δd = vΔt = 2.0 × 6.0 = 12 m.
Step 2. Add where she started. d = 5.0 + 12 = 17 m from the goal.
Step 3. Sense check. She started 5.0 m out and moved 12 m further out, so 17 m is sensible. (A common slip is to answer 12 m and forget she did not start at zero.) ✓
On the graph this is a line that crosses the vertical axis at 5.0 m and climbs 2.0 m for every second.

6In the real world, and hands-on

Fitness app. A running app can draw your distance against time. A steady runner makes a straight line. If the line suddenly flattens, you stopped at a traffic light. If it gets steeper, you sped up.

Skating the Rideau Canal Skateway. At a steady 5 m/s you cover 5 m every second. Plot that and you get a straight line with a slope of 5 m/s. A skater who stops for hot chocolate draws a flat stretch.

Walk it and graph it
  1. Lay out 6 cones in a line, 2 m apart, at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 m.
  2. A partner stands at the 10 m end with a phone stopwatch. Start the clock when you leave the first cone, then walk at a steady pace. The partner says the time as you pass each cone.

Rate yourself honestly. These ratings are for planning the next step, not for marks.

  • I can find the average velocity and average speed for a trip from its graph, and explain why they can differ.
