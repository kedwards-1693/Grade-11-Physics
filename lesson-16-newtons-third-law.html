Newton's third law: forces come in pairs

Why every push has a push back, why the pairs never cancel, and why the apple moves but the Earth does not seem to. About 90 minutes. Split it across two evenings.

Before you start

Have these ready:

  • Calculator
  • Pencil and paper for FBDs
  • Your Newton’s Third Law worksheet, the Third Law questions and the extra problems from Google Classroom

Lessons 12 to 15 first. You will use F = ma again, now on two objects at once.

1The third law

Push on a wall and the wall pushes back on you. Kick a ball and the ball pushes back on your foot. Forces never come alone. They come in pairs.

Newton’s third law

If object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size, opposite in direction, and the same type of force.

F⃗A on B = −F⃗B on A

The key words are on A and on B. Always say which object exerts the force on which object. The worksheet writes this as Fg Earth–physics book: gravity from the Earth on the physics book.

ActionReaction (third-law partner)
Foot kicks the ball with 500 N [N]Ball pushes on the foot with 500 N [S]
Earth pulls down on an apple with 5 NApple pulls up on the Earth with 5 N
Gun pushes the bullet with 1000 N [E]Bullet pushes the gun with 1000 N [W] (recoil)
Book pushes down on the desk with 25 NDesk pushes up on the book with 25 N

2Two mistakes the third law clears up

Mistake 1: “The pair cancels out”

The two forces of a pair act on different objects. You can only add forces that act on the same object, so a pair never cancels. (Mule puzzle: the mule says the cart pulls back on him as hard as he pulls forward, so nothing can move. But the backward pull is on the mule, the forward pull is on the cart. For the cart, the mule’s pull forward beats friction, so it accelerates. For the mule to move, he pushes the ground backward and the ground pushes him forward.)

Mistake 2: “Fn and Fg on a resting object are a third-law pair”

They are equal and opposite, but both act on the same object, and they are different types of force. They are equal because of the first law (a = 0), not the third law. The partner of Fg on the apple is the apple pulling up on the Earth. The partner of Fn on the apple (ground on apple) is the apple pushing down on the ground.

Test for a pair: same type, equal size, opposite direction, and each acts on the object that exerts the other.

4Worked examples with numbers

The third law gives you the size of the force on the second object. F = ma then gives each object’s own acceleration. Same force, different masses, different accelerations.

Worked example 1: the apple and the Earth. The apple has a mass of 0.2 kg. The Earth has a mass of 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg. Find the acceleration of each.

Gravity on the apple: Fg = mg = 0.2 × 9.8 = 1.96 N. By the third law the apple pulls on the Earth with 1.96 N too.
Step 1. Apple. a = F ÷ m = 1.96 ÷ 0.2 = 9.8 m/s² [down].
Step 2. Earth. a = 1.96 ÷ (6.0 × 10²⁴) = 3.3 × 10−25 m/s² [up].
Step 3. Meaning. The force on the Earth is as big as on the apple, but the Earth is so massive that its acceleration is far too small to ever notice.
This is why people think the apple has no effect on the Earth: it does, it is just immeasurably tiny.

Worked example 2: two toboggans (60 kg and 40 kg) are joined by a rope and pulled by a 250 N [E] force on the first one. Ignore friction. Find the acceleration and the tension in the rope between them.

Treat both as one object: m = 100 kg.
Step 1. a = 250 ÷ 100 = 2.5 m/s² [E].
Step 2. Rope tension. The rope pulls only the 40 kg toboggan: T = ma = 40 × 2.5 = 1.0 × 10² N.
Check. The 250 N on the first toboggan minus the 100 N the rope pulls back = 150 N = 60 × 2.5. ✓
The rope pulls forward on the back toboggan and backward on the front one: a third-law pair.

Worked example 3: a rifle (5.0 kg) fires a 50 g bullet along a 0.50 m barrel in 0.010 s. Find the bullet’s acceleration, the force on it, and the recoil of the rifle.

Convert: 50 g = 0.050 kg. Constant acceleration from rest.
Step 1. Bullet acceleration. Δd = ½aΔt² ⇒ a = 2 × 0.50 ÷ 0.010² = 10 000 m/s².
Step 2. Force on the bullet. F = ma = 0.050 × 10 000 = 500 N.
Step 3. Recoil force on the rifle. Third law: 500 N backward.
Step 4. Rifle acceleration. a = 500 ÷ 5.0 = 100 m/s² backward, 100 times smaller because the rifle has 100 times more mass.
Step 5. Distance the rifle moves in 0.010 s. Δd = ½ × 100 × 0.010² = 0.0050 m = 0.5 cm.

Worked example 4: you pull your 17 kg little cousin’s scarf with a 6 N force while you both stand on skates (no friction). How fast is each of you moving after 2.0 s? You have a mass of 60 kg.

Third law: she pulls on you with 6 N too, in the opposite direction.
Step 1. Cousin. a = 6 ÷ 17 = 0.353 m/s². v = at = 0.353 × 2.0 = 0.71 m/s toward you.
Step 2. You. a = 6 ÷ 60 = 0.10 m/s². v = 0.10 × 2.0 = 0.20 m/s, in the opposite direction to your cousin’s motion (the scarf pulls you toward her, and she moves toward you, so you both move toward each other).
Step 3. Answer the doubt. “She holds on, so she can’t pull” is wrong. The scarf is taut, so she is pulling it. Her mass is smaller, but the force on you is just as big as the force on her.
The lighter person ends up with the bigger speed.

5Knowledge check

6Hands-on: push off on chairs

Equal push, different motion
  1. Find two rolling chairs and a smooth floor (or two skateboards with an adult watching, on a flat area). Two people sit facing each other with their feet off the floor.
  2. Push gently on each other’s hands. Notice who moves and how far.
  3. Repeat with one person holding a heavy backpack, so their mass is bigger.
Show a model answer

Both chairs moved apart, even though only one person pushed. With equal masses they moved about the same distance. With the backpack, the heavier person moved less and the lighter one moved more. By the third law, the force on each person was equal and opposite. By F = ma, the same force gives the smaller mass the greater acceleration.

Open Newton’s Third Law (parts A, B, C), the Third Law questions and the three extra problems (child stands then jumps, sliding down a pole, girl and sled). In every question:

  1. Say which object exerts the force on which.
  2. Write the partner force with the same size and the opposite direction.

For the written questions: the bicycle (you need a net force to start it, but only need to balance friction to keep a constant velocity), the mule’s flaw (the pair acts on different objects), and the fireman (the water is pushed forward by the hose, so the hose pushes back on the fireman, and he leans forward to balance it).
