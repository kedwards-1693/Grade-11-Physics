Projectile motion: two motions, one clock

How a kicked or thrown ball moves sideways at a steady speed and up-and-down with gravity at the same time. About 75 minutes, plus a throwing activity.

Before you start

Have these ready:

  • Calculator in degree mode
  • Pencil and paper
  • The SPH3U Formula Sheet from Google Classroom
  • For the activity in part 7: a ball, a tape measure or something to pace out distance, and an open space

Done Lessons 6, 7 and 8? This lesson uses all three: the Big Five, free fall (g) and components.

1Two balls, one fall

Stand on a table. Drop one ball straight down and, at the exact same moment, flick another ball sideways off the edge. Which hits the floor first?

The answer surprises many people: they land at the same time. The sideways ball goes further, but gravity pulls both down in exactly the same way, because sideways motion does not change falling motion. That is the big idea of projectile motion.

The two rules

Horizontal (sideways): nothing speeds it up or slows it down. The velocity is constant, so the simple d = vΔt applies.

Vertical (up and down): gravity gives an acceleration of 9.8 m/s² [down]. The Big Five apply.

The link: both motions happen for the same time Δt. Time is the only quantity the two directions share.

2Try it yourself

A projectile is anything moving through the air under gravity alone (we ignore air resistance). Use the sliders to kick a ball and see how speed and angle change the path.

Things to find out with the sliders:

  • What angle gives the greatest range for a given speed?
  • Pick 30° and then 60°. What do you notice about the two ranges?
  • Double the speed at the same angle. Does the range double?

Answers: 45° gives the greatest range. 30° and 60° give the same range (the dashed grey path shows this). Doubling the speed makes the range four times as long, because the time in the air doubles and the horizontal speed doubles too.

3The method

Horizontal (x)Vertical (y)
Acceleration09.8 m/s² [down]
Equationsdx = vxΔtThe Big Five, with ay = −9.8 (up positive)
Starting velocityvx = v cos θviy = v sin θ
At the topstill vx (unchanged)vy = 0
  1. Sketch and choose positive directions (up for angled launches; down for drops from a height is fine).
  2. Split the launch velocity into vx and viy. (For a horizontal launch, viy = 0.)
  3. Use the vertical equations to find the time Δt. (This is the step people forget.)
  4. Put that Δt into dx = vxΔt for the horizontal answer.
Watch out

Never mix horizontal and vertical numbers in the same equation. The only thing that crosses over is the time. And the horizontal velocity never changes, even at the top of the flight.

4Worked examples

A horizontal launch first, then an angled one.

A ball kicked horizontally off a 20.0 m cliff at 15 m/s.

Worked example 1: a ball is kicked horizontally at 15 m/s off the top of a 20.0 m cliff. How far from the base does it land? How fast is it moving as it lands?

Sketch: down is positive. Horizontal: vx = 15 m/s. Vertical: viy = 0 (horizontal launch); ay = +9.8 m/s²; Δdy = +20.0 m.
Step 1. Find the time (vertical). vfy is neither given nor wanted: equation 3 with viy = 0. Δdy = ½ayΔt².
Step 2. Solve. Δt = √(2 × 20.0 ÷ 9.8) = 2.02 s.
Step 3. Horizontal distance. Δdx = vxΔt = 15 × 2.02 = 30.3, so 30. m (2 digits).
Step 4. Landing velocity components. Horizontal is still 15 m/s. Vertical: vfy = viy + ayΔt = 0 + 9.8 × 2.02 = 19.8 m/s [down].
Components stay separate until the very end.
Step 5. Combine. Speed = √(15² + 19.8²) = 24.8, so 25 m/s. Angle = tan−1(19.8 ÷ 15) = 53° below the horizontal: [E 53° D].
Step 6. Sense check. 30 m sideways from a 20 m drop is believable. The landing speed is more than the starting speed, because gravity added downward speed. ✓

A ball kicked at 22 m/s, 35° above the ground, landing at its starting height.

Worked example 2: a ball is kicked at 22 m/s [E 35° N] from the ground. Find the time in the air, the range and the maximum height.

Sketch: up is positive. From Lesson 8: vx = 22 cos 35° = 18.0 m/s and viy = 22 sin 35° = 12.6 m/s. ay = −9.8 m/s². It returns to its starting level, so Δdy = 0.
Step 1. Time of flight (vertical). Use Δdy = viyΔt + ½ayΔt² with Δdy = 0. That gives Δt = 2viy ÷ 9.8.
Step 2. Solve. Δt = 2 × 12.6 ÷ 9.8 = 2.575, so 2.6 s.
Step 3. Range (horizontal). Δdx = vxΔt = 18.0 × 2.575 = 46.4, so 46 m.
Step 4. Maximum height. At the top vfy = 0, so use equation 5: Δdy = (0 − 12.6²) ÷ (2 × −9.8) = 8.1 m.
The ball reaches the top after half the time of flight (1.3 s). Check: 12.6 ÷ 9.8 = 1.29 s.
Step 5. Sense check. A good kick: about 46 m, which is half a football pitch. The height is a lot less than the range, as expected for a 35° kick. ✓

Worked example 3: a football is kicked at 35° above the horizontal. It takes 2.1 s to return to its starting height. What was its launch speed, and how far did it travel horizontally?

Given: θ = 35°; Δt = 2.1 s; Δdy = 0. Unknown: v and the range.
Step 1. Vertical first. Δdy = 0 = viy(2.1) + ½(−9.8)(2.1)², so viy = 4.9 × 2.1 = 10.3 m/s.
Step 2. Launch speed. viy = v sin 35°, so v = 10.3 ÷ sin 35° = 17.9, so 18 m/s.
Step 3. Horizontal speed. vx = 17.9 cos 35° = 14.7 m/s.
Step 4. Range. Δdx = 14.7 × 2.1 = 30.9, so 31 m.

Worked example 4: with a launch speed of 20 m/s on level ground, a range of 23.5 m can be reached with two different angles. Find them.

The range on level ground is R = v2 sin(2θ) ÷ g. (Derived by combining the time of flight and the horizontal speed.)
Step 1. Rearrange. sin(2θ) = Rg ÷ v² = (23.5 × 9.8) ÷ 20² = 0.576.
Step 2. First angle. 2θ = sin−1(0.576) = 35.2°, so θ = 17.6°.
Step 3. Second angle. The complement: 90° − 17.6° = 72.4°.
Angles that add to 90° give the same range. The sliders in part 2 show this. The greatest range of all is at 45°, where sin(2θ) = 1.

6In the real world

Soccer. A goal kick, a corner kick and a throw-in are all projectiles. A long goal kick uses an angle a bit below 45° in practice, because air resistance and the ball spin matter. A chip over a goalkeeper uses a high angle, and a low drive uses a low one.

Basketball. A free throw is a projectile. A higher arc means a steeper entry angle into the hoop, which gives a bigger target.

Engineers. Water fountains, sprinklers and even stunt ramps are designed with projectile calculations.

7Hands-on: find the best angle

Throw or kick at three angles
  1. Use an open space. Mark a throwing line. Pick an angle guide: 30° is a low arc, 45° is halfway between flat and straight up, and 60° is a high arc.
  2. Throw or kick the ball with the same effort at each of the three angles. Do 3 tries at each angle.
  3. Pace out or measure the distance for each try. Type the average for each angle below.
  4. Which angle went the farthest? What did you notice about 30° and 60°?
