Gravity and free fall: the Big Five meet a falling ball

Why everything falls with the same acceleration, how to pick a sign, and how to solve up-and-down problems. About 60 minutes, plus a reaction-time activity.

Before you start

Have these ready:

  • Calculator, pencil and paper
  • The SPH3U Formula Sheet (g = 9.8 m/s² [down] is at the top of the kinematics part)
  • For the activity in part 6: a 30 cm ruler (or any ruler), and a partner

Done Lesson 6? This lesson is the Big Five with a = 9.8 m/s² plugged in. Use the same routine.

1Hammer and feather

In 1971, on the Moon, astronaut David Scott held a hammer and a feather at the same height and let go at the same time. They hit the ground together. There is no air on the Moon, so nothing pushed back on the feather.

On Earth, air does push back, so a feather falls slowly. But take away the air (or use a compact ball and a heavy ball) and everything falls the same way. The heavy one is pulled harder, but it is also harder to move, and the two effects cancel exactly.

2The one number: g

In plain words

Free fall means gravity is the only thing affecting the motion. Then every object has the same acceleration:

g = 9.8 m/s² [down]

That is true whether the object is dropped, thrown up, thrown down, rising, falling or sitting at the top for an instant. The acceleration never changes and never takes a break.

1 s9.8 m/s4.9 m

The speed grows by 9.8 m/s every second, but the distance grows faster and faster. (Look at 4.9, then 19.6, then 44.1.)

Watch out

Pick a sign and stick with it. If up is positive, then g is −9.8 m/s². If down is positive, then g is +9.8 m/s². Both work, but only if you stay consistent. For a ball thrown up I recommend up = positive. For something just dropped, down = positive is easier, because everything comes out positive.

Two things people get wrong all the time:

  • “Heavier things fall faster.” Not without air. Same g for everything.
  • “At the top of its flight, the acceleration is zero.” No. The velocity is zero for an instant, but gravity is still there at 9.8 m/s² [down]. If the acceleration really were zero at the top, the ball would hang there forever.

3Up-and-down motion

Take a ball thrown straight up at 14 m/s, with up as positive. Its velocity falls steadily, from +14 m/s to 0 at the top, and then on to −14 m/s when it returns:

Same motion, two graphs. The height-time curve is a hill (position). The velocity-time graph is a straight line sloping down with a slope of −9.8 m/s². The line crosses zero at the top.

  • At the top: v = 0 and a = −9.8 m/s². This is the key fact for most problems.
  • Symmetry: the time going up equals the time coming down (to the same height).
  • Same speed at the same height: the ball passes any height going up with the same speed it has going down there, just in the opposite direction.

4Worked examples

Worked example 1: a ball is dropped from a 20.0 m roof. How long does it take to land, and how fast is it going when it hits? (Ignore air.)

Sketch: down is positive. Given: vi = 0 (dropped); a = +9.8 m/s²; Δd = +20.0 m. Unknowns: Δt, then vf.
Time. vf is neither given nor wanted, so use equation 3. With vi = 0: Δd = ½aΔt².
Rearrange. Δt = √(2Δd ÷ a) = √(2 × 20.0 ÷ 9.8) = √4.08 = 2.02 s.
Speed. Now the wanted quantity is vf. Δt is not needed, so use equation 5 with the original givens: vf² = 0 + 2(9.8)(20.0) = 392.
Solve. vf = √392 = 19.8 m/s [down].
Sense check. 19.8 m/s is about 71 km/h, which is believable for a 20 m (6-storey) fall. Check: 9.8 × 2.02 = 19.8. ✓

Worked example 2: a ball is thrown straight up at 14 m/s. How high does it go, and how long until it returns to the thrower’s hand?

Sketch: up is positive. Given: vi = +14 m/s; a = −9.8 m/s²; at the top vf = 0 (hidden given). Unknowns: Δd (top), Δt.
Height. Given vi, vf and a, and wanting Δd: Δt is neither, so use equation 5.
Solve. Δd = (0 − 14²) ÷ (2 × −9.8) = −196 ÷ −19.6 = 10 m above the start.
Time up. Δd is not needed: equation 1. 0 = 14 + (−9.8)Δt, so Δt = 14 ÷ 9.8 = 1.43 s.
Total time. Symmetry: it takes the same time to come down. Total = 2 × 1.43 = 2.9 s.
The ball returns at v = −14 m/s: the same speed, going down.
Sense check. 10 m up at a launch speed of 14 m/s (about 50 km/h). Believable. ✓

Worked example 3: a ball is thrown down from a bridge at 5.0 m/s and falls 12 m. How fast is it going when it lands?

Sketch: down is positive. Given: vi = +5.0 m/s; a = +9.8 m/s²; Δd = +12 m. Unknown: vf.
Pick the equation. Δt is neither given nor wanted: equation 5.
Substitute. vf² = 5.0² + 2(9.8)(12) = 25 + 235 = 260.
Solve. vf = √260 = 16.1, so 16 m/s [down].
Sense check. A little faster than a dropped ball would be (15.3 m/s from 12 m), because it started with 5.0 m/s. ✓

6In the real world, and hands-on

Diving. A diver leaves a 10 m platform from rest. The fall time is √(2 × 10 ÷ 9.8) = 1.4 s, and she hits the water at about 14 m/s (50 km/h). That is why a belly flop hurts.

Goalkeepers. A goalkeeper’s goal kick that goes straight up spends a longer time in the air than you expect. Twice the launch speed gives twice the time up, but four times the height.

The ruler drop: measure your reaction time
  1. Your partner holds a ruler by the top end, so that the zero end hangs between your thumb and finger without touching.
  2. Your partner drops it without warning. You catch it as fast as you can.
  3. Read the distance the ruler fell (where your thumb is) in centimetres. Do 3 drops.
  4. The ruler starts from rest and falls freely, so Δd = ½gΔt². Rearranged, Δt = √(2Δd ÷ 9.8), with Δd in metres (divide cm by 100).
